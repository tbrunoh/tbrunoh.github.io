Africa's farm data is open. The last mile to farmers is the work left to do

Forecasts, rainfall records and soil maps for Africa are free and good. Getting them to a farmer as a decision she can act on is the work left to do.

By Tom Bruno · 24 November 2026 · 4 minute read

In 2025, 38 million farmers in India received a forecast on their phones of when the monsoon would arrive, up to four weeks ahead. India's Ministry of Agriculture and Farmers' Welfare sent it, using AI weather models that a University of Chicago team had tested for one of the biggest decisions farmers make: when to plant. The forecast even caught a rare stall: the monsoon advanced for about a week, then stopped for close to three weeks. The Nobel laureate economist Michael Kremer estimates that such forecasts likely return more than $100 to farmers for every dollar a government invests.

African farmers deserve the same. Most of the raw material already exists, and much of it is free.

The data is now open, and it is good

Public resourceWhat it offers a farmerAccess
ECMWF AIFS, an AI weather modelGlobal forecasts, operational since February 2025, with an ensemble since July 2025Free open data
CHIRPS rainfall recordDaily rainfall from 1981 to the present at about 5 km, the baseline for knowing when the rains usually startPublic domain
iSDAsoilSoil properties across Africa at 30 m, the scale of a smallholder's field, trained on more than 100,000 soil samplesOpen access

WAXAL speech dataOpen speech data in 27 Sub-Saharan African languages, for voice answers in Hausa, Yoruba or LugandaCC BY 4.0

Each of these is free to use today. An advisory service no longer has to buy or build its own forecasts, rainfall history or soil maps before it can help a single farmer.

The last mile is the hard part

None of this reaches a farmer by itself. Three things stand between the data and a decision she can act on.

  1. Turning data into a decision. A farmer needs to know whether to plant this week, and a probability map leaves that work to her. The India programme chose its models by how well they predicted the decision that mattered, the start of the monsoon, rather than by forecast skill in general.
  2. A channel she already uses. GSMA finds that only 28% of Africans use mobile internet on their own devices. For most farmers, advice has to arrive by text, voice or a short code menu, not in an app.
  3. Trust. The answer must say where it came from and how sure it is, and it must never be confidently wrong about something that can hurt her. Human extension services are stretched: in Kenya, the Daily Nation reports that public extension officers are dwindling while the number of farmers who need technical advice grows. Digital advice has to earn the trust those officers built.

The demand is real. Digital Green's FarmerChat reaches nearly 3 million farmers, and Digital Green reports that around seventy percent of them act on the advice they receive.

What a good last mile looks like

A farmer in Kitui gets a text in Swahili on a Thursday: rain is likely on Friday and Saturday, dry from Sunday; plant after the first soaking rain. One line underneath says which forecast it came from and when. If she replies with a question about armyworm, the answer cites FAO and ICIPE guidance. If she texts about an emergency, she gets her own country's emergency numbers before anything else.

That message works on any phone, and every line in it can be checked.

Build the last mile once, and share it

Today, most advisory services in Africa rebuild the same pipes: fetching forecasts, cleaning soil data, writing decision rules, handling SMS and USSD, and checking safety. That spends scarce money on plumbing and leaves each service with its own blind spots. The open, dated and cited layer should be built once and shared.

That is what V2 of ACE, the open source African Context Engine, sets out to do: turn public weather, flood, soil and health data into short decisions on any phone, each with its source and date. The first six services shipped on 24 November 2026 as an open source preview, covering ACE's countries wherever open data exists, 34 of them since release 2.1: rain this week, official warnings, the nearest health facility, market prices, a pregnancy companion and a crop problem check. The planting window, flood warnings and soil advice come next.

If you run a met service, an extension programme, a telecom or an advisory service, the open data is ready for you to use. ACE is open, and I would welcome partners in any of its 34 countries who want to test the last mile with farmers.
