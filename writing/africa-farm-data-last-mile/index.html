Africa's farm data is open. The last mile to farmers is the work left to do

Forecasts, rainfall records and soil maps for Africa are free and good. Getting them to a farmer as a decision she can act on is the work left to do.

By Tom Bruno · 24 November 2026 · 3 minute read

In 2025, 38 million farmers in India received a forecast on their phones of when the monsoon would arrive, up to four weeks ahead. India's Ministry of Agriculture and Farmers' Welfare sent it, using AI weather models that a University of Chicago team had tested for one of the biggest decisions farmers make: when to plant. The forecast even caught an unusual pause of several weeks in the rains. The Nobel laureate economist Michael Kremer estimates that such forecasts likely return more than $100 to farmers for every dollar a government invests.

African farmers deserve the same. Most of the raw material already exists, and much of it is free.

The data is now open, and it is good

Public resourceWhat it offers a farmerAccess
ECMWF AIFS, an AI weather modelGlobal forecasts, operational since February 2025, with an ensemble since July 2025Free open data
CHIRPS rainfall recordDaily rainfall from 1981 to the present at about 5 km, the baseline for knowing when the rains usually startPublic domain
iSDAsoilSoil properties across Africa at 30 m, the scale of a smallholder's field, trained on more than 100,000 soil samplesOpen access
Google Flood HubRiverine flood forecasts up to 7 days ahead, in more than 150 countriesFree and public
WAXAL speech dataOpen speech data in more than 20 African languages, for voice answers in Hausa, Yoruba or LugandaCC BY 4.0

Each of these is free to use today. An advisory service no longer has to buy or build its own forecasts, rainfall history or soil maps before it can help a single farmer.

The last mile is the hard part

None of this reaches a farmer by itself. A forecast grid is not advice. Three things stand between the data and a decision.

  1. Turning data into a decision. A farmer does not need a probability map. She needs to know whether to plant this week. The India programme worked because its models were judged on the decision that mattered, the start of the monsoon, not on forecast skill in general.
  2. A channel she already uses. GSMA finds that only 28% of Africans use mobile internet on their own devices. For most farmers, advice has to arrive by text, voice or a short code menu, not in an app.
  3. Trust. The answer must say where it came from and how sure it is, and it must never be confidently wrong about something that can hurt her. Human extension services are stretched: in Kenya, the Daily Nation reports that public extension officers are dwindling while the number of farmers who need technical advice grows. Digital advice has to earn the trust those officers built.

Demand is not in doubt. Digital Green's FarmerChat reaches nearly 3 million farmers, and around seventy percent of them act on the advice they receive.

What a good last mile looks like

Picture a text that arrives in Swahili on a Thursday: rain is likely on Friday and Saturday, dry from Sunday; plant after the first soaking rain. One line underneath says which forecast it came from and when. If she replies with a question about armyworm, the answer cites FAO and ICIPE guidance. If she texts about an emergency, she gets her own country's emergency numbers before anything else.

Nothing in that message needs a smartphone. Everything in it can be checked.

Build the last mile once, and share it

Today, every advisory service in Africa rebuilds the same pipes: fetching forecasts, cleaning soil data, writing decision rules, handling SMS and USSD, and checking safety. That spends scarce money on plumbing and leaves each service with its own blind spots. The open, dated and cited layer should be built once and shared.

That is what the V2 design of ACE, the open source African Context Engine, sets out to do. It turns public weather, flood, soil and health data and open African speech models into twelve decision services, from rain this week and a planting window to flood warnings and soil advice, each answering on any phone with its source and date. The design is published, and the build starts in December 2026.

If you run a met service, an extension programme, a telecom or an advisory service, the data is no longer the constraint. The last mile is. The ACE design is open, and I would welcome partners who want to test it with farmers.
