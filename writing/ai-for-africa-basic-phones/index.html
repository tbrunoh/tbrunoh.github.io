AI for Africa has to work on the phone people already own

Almost a billion Africans live within reach of mobile internet and still do not use it. AI that only works in an app will miss them.

By Tom Bruno · 24 November 2026 · 4 minute read

Most conversations about AI in Africa start with a smartphone app. That is the wrong place to start. The people who would gain the most from good advice today, a farmer watching caterpillars eat her maize or a mother who has just received a mobile money message that feels wrong, are often holding a phone that cannot run an app at all.

The gap is use, not coverage

GSMA's Mobile Economy Africa 2026 report finds that 63% of Africans live within mobile broadband coverage but do not use mobile internet. Only 9% live outside coverage. That is almost a billion people within reach of the network and outside the apps. GSMA's Digital Africa Index 2026 puts it another way: networks cover 92% of Africa's population, yet only 28% use mobile internet on their own devices.

The main barrier is the device. By the end of 2025, an entry level internet enabled handset cost the poorest fifth of people in Sub-Saharan Africa the equivalent of 76% of their average monthly income, according to GSMA. Since then, memory prices have more than doubled, and entry level smartphone prices are rising, not falling. Waiting for everyone to own a smartphone means waiting a long time.

The channels that already work

The channels that need no smartphone and no data plan already carry Africa's most important digital service. Mobile money runs on short code menus and text messages that work on any handset. More than $2 trillion moved through mobile money worldwide in 2025, and most of its 593 million monthly active accounts are in Sub-Saharan Africa. People already trust a menu and a text message with their money. The same channels can carry advice.

The proof is in market today:

  • Health by text. Jacaranda Health's PROMPTS uses two way SMS to support mothers in Kenya through pregnancy and after birth, and has reached more than 3.8 million of them.
  • AI by voice call. MTN Rwanda and Viamo launched Miss Baza, a voice AI that MTN customers reach by dialling 845. It answers out loud in Kinyarwanda, English, French and Kiswahili, with no app or data bundle, and has reached more than 500,000 users.
  • Farm advice at scale. Digital Green's FarmerChat reaches nearly 3 million farmers across six countries in more than ten languages.

What changes when AI runs on a basic phone

Building for USSD, SMS and voice is not a smaller version of building an app. It changes the design.

  • Every word costs. A USSD screen holds at most 182 characters, and an SMS reply should fit in a few segments. The answer has to be the instruction, not an essay.
  • The source travels with the answer. There is no link to tap and no page of fine print. Where the advice came from has to fit in one line the person can read.
  • Safety comes first, literally. When someone texts that she is bleeding, the right emergency number for her country has to arrive before anything else. A confident model that gives a number from the wrong country is worse than no answer.
  • Language is the interface. Africa is home to more than 30% of the world's languages, while leading AI models are trained mostly on English and other high resource languages, GSMA notes. Open data is starting to close that gap: WAXAL, an open speech dataset for more than 20 Sub-Saharan African languages spoken by over 100 million people, is free to use under CC BY 4.0.

Knowledge is the bottleneck, not the model

The hard part is not the model. It is the knowledge underneath it: which hotline handles gender based violence in Ghana, what moisture level keeps maize free of aflatoxin, how a real mobile money message differs from a scam. Global models do not know these facts reliably. Every service above has had to build that knowledge, its channels and its safety layer on its own, and the knowledge then stays locked inside each one.

That is why I built ACE, the African Context Engine, as open source. ACE serves checked, citable knowledge over USSD, SMS, voice, WhatsApp, the web and MCP, in eight languages across 34 countries, from Swahili and French to Arabic and Amharic. The caller's dialling code picks the country. Emergencies get that country's verified numbers before anything else runs, and every answer names its source. ACE works with no AI model at all, and when one is added, it checks that the model stayed with the facts.

The test for AI in Africa

The test I use is simple. Could a farmer in Kakamega or a trader in Kano use it today, on the phone in her hand, in her language, without mobile data? If not, the product is not yet built for Africa. If so, the next question is whether every answer can be traced to a source she would trust.

If you know a place, a language or a channel, ACE is open for contributors.
