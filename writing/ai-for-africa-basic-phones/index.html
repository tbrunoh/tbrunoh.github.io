AI for Africa has to work on the phone people already own

Almost a billion Africans live within reach of mobile internet and still do not use it. AI that only works in an app will miss them.

By Tom Bruno · 24 November 2026 · Updated 25 November 2026 · 4 minute read

Most conversations about AI in Africa begin with a smartphone app. The people who would gain most from good advice today, a farmer watching caterpillars eat her maize or a mother reading a mobile money message that feels wrong, often hold a phone that cannot run an app at all.

The network reaches people the apps do not

Mobile broadband now covers 92% of Africa's population, yet only 28% of people use mobile internet on their own devices, according to the GSMA's Digital Africa Index 2026. That 64% usage gap is almost a billion people who live within reach of the network and outside the apps.

The device is a large part of the reason. At the end of 2025, an entry level internet enabled handset cost the poorest fifth of people in Sub-Saharan Africa 76% of their average monthly income, GSMA reports. Memory prices then more than doubled between late 2025 and early 2026, and entry level phones are getting more expensive. A plan that waits for everyone to own a smartphone will wait a long time.

The channels that already work

Africa's most important digital service already runs without a smartphone or a data plan. Mobile money works through short code menus and text messages on any handset. More than $2 trillion moved through mobile money wallets worldwide in 2025, and most new accounts came from Sub-Saharan Africa. People already trust a menu and a text message with their money. Those same channels can carry advice.

Services like these are proving the demand:

  • Health by text. Jacaranda Health's PROMPTS sends pregnancy and newborn advice by SMS and answers mothers' questions. It has reached more than 3.8 million mothers in Kenya and launched in Kano, Nigeria, in March 2026.
  • AI by voice call. MTN Rwanda and Viamo launched Miss Baza, a voice AI that MTN customers reach by dialling 845. It answers out loud in Kinyarwanda, English, French and Kiswahili, with no app or data bundle, and has reached more than 500,000 users.
  • Farm advice at scale. Digital Green's FarmerChat, an app and chat service, reaches nearly 3 million farmers across six countries, including India, in more than ten languages.

Designing for the basic phone

Building for USSD, SMS and voice changes the design from the first screen.

  • Every word costs. A USSD screen holds at most 182 characters, and an SMS reply should fit in a few segments. The answer has to be the instruction itself.
  • The source travels with the answer. There is no link to tap and no page of fine print. Where the advice came from has to fit in one line the person can read.
  • Safety comes first. When someone texts that she is bleeding, the right emergency number for her country has to arrive before anything else. A confident model that gives another country's number does real harm.
  • Language is the interface. Africa is home to more than 30% of the world's languages, while today's leading AI models are trained mostly on English and other high resource languages, GSMA notes. Open data is starting to close that gap: WAXAL, an open speech dataset from Google Research and African partners, covers 27 Sub-Saharan African languages spoken by over 100 million people, released under CC BY 4.0 or CC BY-SA 4.0 depending on the partner that collected each language.

The missing layer is local knowledge

What global models lack is the knowledge underneath a good answer: which hotline handles gender based violence in Ghana, what moisture level keeps maize free of aflatoxin, how a real mobile money message differs from a scam. They do not know these facts reliably. Each service above has built that knowledge, its channels and its safety layer on its own, and the knowledge stays locked inside each one.

That is why I built ACE, the African Context Engine, as open source. ACE serves checked, citable knowledge on any phone (USSD, SMS/RCS, voice, WhatsApp and MCP), in 32 languages across 34 countries, from Swahili, Hausa and isiZulu to Arabic and Amharic. The caller's dialling code picks the country. Emergencies get that country's verified numbers before anything else runs, and every answer names its source. ACE works with no AI model at all, and when one is added, it checks that the model stayed with the facts.

The test for AI in Africa

The test I use is simple. Could a farmer in Kakamega or a trader in Kano use it today, on the phone in her hand, in her language, without mobile data? If not, the product is not yet built for Africa. If so, the next question is whether every answer can be traced to a source she would trust.

If you know a place, a language or a channel, ACE is open for contributors.
